# Company Notebook: explain a real business to a first-time reader

The intended reader is beginning an investing journey and may never have encountered this industry. Apply this standard to every new company, company addition, content update and financial explanation.

- Start with recognisable products or a concrete service. For unfamiliar businesses, describe the actual object or work: what people, machines or software do and what problem this solves for a customer.
- Say who the customer is, who pays, and what the payment buys. Distinguish a service fee, sale of goods, subscription, interest and other income where relevant. Do not assume the user understands how a business earns money from its industry label.
- Explain the main costs and a simple sequence from work or delivery to the bill and customer payment. Revenue, profit and cash received are different things.
- Prefer everyday verbs: unload, store, move, build, connect, test, lend, collect and repair. Phrases such as “integrated solutions”, “execution”, “platform”, “logistics” or “material handling” need a concrete explanation rather than another abstract synonym.
- Define essential technical words and abbreviations at first use, where the reader encounters them. Do not rely on a separate glossary or assume a familiar brand makes the underlying business obvious.
- Apply the same clarity to product/service image captions and research questions. Keep an official technical name when useful, but describe what the item does in everyday words beside it.
- Use a clearly labelled illustrative situation when it helps. Do not invent a real customer, contract, price, fee or revenue source. Retain source-supported company boundaries and the distinction between present and discontinued businesses.
- Build from basic understanding to company-specific questions and risks, then actual financial figures at the end. Preserve verified financial definitions, sources, dates and reporting scope. Never invent product or brand profit.
- Rewrite unclear text instead of stacking another repetitive explanation on top. The one-minute overview should be a short orientation; deeper sections must add concrete understanding.

Before publishing, read the opening as someone unfamiliar with the industry. They should be able to answer: What does the company actually do? Who pays it and for what? What costs money? What could make the business harder? Explain any word required to answer those questions.
